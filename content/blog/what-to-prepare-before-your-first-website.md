## Start with what the website needs to do

Before any design or copywriting starts, it helps to be clear on the
website's job. Is it mainly to generate new leads, to give existing
customers information like hours and menus, to support online ordering or
bookings, or to build credibility for a business that currently only exists
on social media? Different goals lead to different page structures,
different priorities, and different content needs — so settling this first
saves time later.

## Gather the basics

A few things are worth having ready before a project starts:

- **Business name, logo, and any existing brand colors or fonts** — if a
  brand hasn't been formalized yet, that's fine too, but it's worth flagging
  up front.
- **Photos and video**, if available — real photos of the business, team,
  location, or product generally read as more trustworthy than stock imagery.
- **Core pages of copy** — even rough notes on what each page should say
  (services, about, FAQs) give a project a head start.
- **Existing accounts to connect** — booking systems, ordering platforms,
  payment processors, email marketing tools, or social accounts the new site
  should link to or integrate with.
- **A domain name**, if one is already owned, or a short list of name ideas
  if not.

## Decide on scope before getting quotes

"I want a website" can mean a single-page site or a multi-page platform with
custom tools — and the gap in cost and timeline between those is large. It
helps to think through:

- How many distinct pages or sections are actually needed
- Whether the business needs ongoing updates (a blog, changing promotions,
  new products) or a mostly static site
- Whether multilingual support matters for the audience
- Whether specific integrations (reservations, online ordering, scheduling)
  are required on day one or can come later

Walking into a conversation with answers to these questions — even
approximate ones — makes it much easier to get an accurate quote and a
realistic timeline.

## Think about who maintains it after launch

A website isn't a one-time purchase; it needs updates, security patches,
and occasional content changes once it's live. Before starting, it's worth
deciding whether that will be handled in-house, by whoever built the site,
or some mix of both — see [why working directly with one person on both the
build and the ongoing care can matter](/why-sir).
