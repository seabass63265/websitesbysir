## The menu has to be easy to find and read

For most restaurant websites, the menu is the single most-visited page. It
should be reachable in one tap from the homepage, easy to read on a phone
without pinching to zoom, and organized the way the restaurant actually
presents its food — by course, by category, or however makes sense for the
menu itself. A PDF buried in a "Menu" link that forces visitors to download
and zoom is a common and avoidable source of frustration.

## Ordering and reservations should be one click away

If a restaurant offers online ordering, delivery, or reservations through a
platform like Toast, DoorDash, Grubhub, Uber Eats, OpenTable, or Resy, those
links deserve prominent placement — not buried in a footer. The goal is to
get a hungry visitor from "I found this restaurant" to "I placed an order"
or "I booked a table" with as few steps as possible.

## Location, hours, and contact info need to be unmistakable

It sounds basic, but address, hours, phone number, and parking or
accessibility details should be easy to find from any page, not just a
dedicated "Contact" page. A lot of restaurant site visits come from someone
standing outside deciding whether to walk in, or searching on their phone
while already nearby — the site should answer those questions instantly.

## Photos do a lot of the selling

Good photos of food, the dining room, and the team tend to matter more for
restaurants than almost any other type of business — people are deciding
whether they want to eat there, and a strong gallery page or well-placed
photography throughout the site does real work toward that decision.

## Don't forget specials, events, and seasonal updates

Happy hours, seasonal menus, holiday hours, and private event information
all need a home on the site, and ideally a way to update them without
waiting weeks for a developer. For a closer look at what a build like this
typically includes, see the [Restaurants & Cafés
page](/industries/restaurants-and-cafes).
